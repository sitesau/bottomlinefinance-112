Construction Loans Comparison Explained

When evaluating construction loans, developers must distinguish between a standalone senior facility and a comprehensive development funding stack. A senior construction loan is designed specifically to fund the build phase, releasing capital in stages as work progresses. This contrasts with broader development finance, which might layer mezzanine debt or preferred equity behind the senior tranche to maximise total leverage. The choice between these structures fundamentally alters the cost of capital and the equity required from the developer.

Senior facilities are generally preferred when the project has strong presales and modest leverage requirements. The pricing reflects the lower risk profile associated with a secured, first-ranking position over the build assets. Conversely, a layered stack becomes necessary when the feasibility model requires higher gearing than a senior lender will provide alone. While this increases the total cost of capital due to the higher return demanded by mezzanine providers, it reduces the upfront equity injection required from the developer. Lenders often hold back a retention amount until practical completion to ensure the builder finishes all minor works.

Bank Policy vs Non-Bank Lending Flexibility

The comparison between major banks and non-bank lenders is a critical component of any loan assessment. Major banks typically apply conservative policies regarding presale cover and maximum leverage ratios. They often require a high percentage of the project to be sold before the first draw is released. This approach minimizes the lender's risk but can constrain the developer's cash flow and equity efficiency.

Non-bank lenders assess deals on their merits rather than fitting them into a rigid credit box. They can consider scenarios with reduced or nil presale cover, provided the feasibility and exit strategy are robust. This flexibility is particularly valuable for developers working on tighter build programs or those aiming to preserve equity for future projects. However, this flexibility comes with a price, as the cost of funds from non-bank sources is typically higher to reflect the increased risk tolerance. As noted in industry guidance, the cheapest rate is rarely the best outcome if the structure prevents the deal from proceeding or erodes the return on equity too aggressively.

Gearing and Feasibility Metrics

Gearing metrics provide a clearer picture of project feasibility than a single loan-to-value ratio. Lenders commonly use the loan to total development cost and the loan to gross realisable value. The LTC measures the debt against the hard and soft costs of the project, while the GRV measures the debt against the estimated end value of the completed asset. A healthy project must satisfy both metrics to ensure the debt is serviceable.

Interest structures also differ between products. In many commercial construction facilities, interest is capitalised within the loan during the build period. This means the borrower does not make monthly interest payments out of pocket; instead, the interest is added to the loan balance and settled at practical completion or refinance. This approach assists with cash flow management during the construction phase when the property is not generating income. The ability to capitalise interest helps preserve working capital for other aspects of the development.

Progress Draw Mechanics

Progress draw mechanics are central to how construction loans operate. Rather than receiving the full lump sum upfront, the borrower requests funds as specific build stages are completed. These stages usually correspond to the slab down, frame lock-up, and practical completion. A quantity surveyor typically verifies that the work has been done to the required standard before the lender releases the funds.

This staged release mechanism aligns the lender's exposure with the physical value of the asset. It ensures that capital is only deployed when there is tangible progress on the ground. It also provides a degree of interest efficiency, as the borrower generally only pays interest on the amount that has been drawn down, rather than the total facility limit. This method mitigates the risk for the lender that funds might be diverted or that the project stalls before completion.

Exit Strategies and Refinancing

Finally, the exit strategy remains a focal point for lenders comparing different loan options. A senior facility might be repaid through a refinance into a residual stock loan once the build is complete. Alternatively, a layered development stack might rely on a bulk sale or individual settlements to pay out the debt. Lenders scrutinise the exit path closely because it represents the moment the principal is returned.

Developers must present a realistic timeline for this exit. If the market conditions suggest a longer sell-down period, the loan structure must accommodate the increased holding costs. A strong, well-articulated exit strategy can often unlock better pricing and leverage terms, regardless of whether the lender is a major bank or a non-bank specialist. The clarity of the exit is often as important as the raw numbers in the feasibility study.

  1. Assess Feasibility. Prepare a detailed feasibility study including total development cost and gross realisable value to determine your leverage needs.
  2. Determine Equity. Calculate how much equity you can contribute and what level of presale cover is realistically achievable before construction starts.
  3. Select Structure. Decide between a senior facility and a layered stack based on your required leverage and risk tolerance.
  4. Engage Lenders. Approach lenders that match the project's profile, choosing between banks for lower costs or non-banks for flexibility.

Common questions

What is the main difference between a construction loan and development finance? A construction loan funds the build phase specifically, while development finance is a broader term that can include land acquisition and multiple layers of debt like mezzanine finance to maximise leverage.

Why would a developer choose a non-bank lender over a bank? Developers choose non-bank lenders when they need higher leverage, faster approval, or have lower presale cover than bank policy allows, accepting a higher cost of capital for this flexibility.

How are funds released during the construction process? Funds are released in progress draws, triggered by the completion of specific build stages such as slab down or frame lock-up, which are verified by a quantity surveyor.

This comparison applies to commercial and business-purpose construction funding in Australia, not owner-occupied home loans.
