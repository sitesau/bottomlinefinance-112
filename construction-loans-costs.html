Construction Loans Costs Explained

The cost of a construction facility is not static. It shifts based on several key variables including the loan to cost ratio, the loan to value against end value and the level of presale cover. Pricing is also influenced by the strength of the builder and the clarity of the takeout or sell-down strategy. Stronger presale cover and lower gearing generally lead to keener pricing. Conversely, a structure that requires higher leverage or has low presales carries a higher cost. Lenders assess these factors to determine the risk profile of the deal. When assessing options for construction loans, developers must weigh these variables against their project's specific feasibility.

Gearing is measured against two distinct metrics: the loan to total development cost (LTC) and the loan to value against the gross realisable value (GRV). A facility that tracks both metrics provides a clearer picture of feasibility than a single loan-to-value ratio snapshot. Lenders require a clear exit strategy, whether through presales, a refinance to residual stock finance or a bulk sale, to ensure the facility can be repaid.

Progress payments and interest efficiency

Unlike standard mortgages where funds are drawn as a lump sum, construction finance is released in stages. This progress draw structure aligns with the build program. Each draw is released when a defined build stage is reached, usually confirmed by a quantity surveyor. This method provides interest efficiency because the borrower only pays interest on the amount that has been drawn down, rather than the full facility limit.

Interest is commonly capitalised within the facility across the construction period. This means the interest accrues and is added to the loan balance rather than being paid monthly in cash. This arrangement assists with cash flow during the build phase, as no monthly principal or interest repayments are usually required until the project is completed or the facility expires. Lenders also typically hold a retention amount from each draw, releasing it only upon practical completion.

Bank versus non-bank costs

Major banks typically apply conservative policies regarding presale requirements and leverage caps. When a project falls outside these standard parameters, non-bank lenders assess the deal on its merits. Non-bank options are often necessary for situations requiring higher leverage than bank policy allows or where presale cover is below the threshold a major bank requires. These lenders may also accommodate tighter build programs or non-standard project structures.

However, this flexibility usually comes at a price. Non-bank lenders price their products to reflect the higher risk tolerance and the bespoke nature of the funding. While the cost of capital is generally higher than a standard bank loan, the decision to use a non-bank lender often hinges on whether the return on equity justifies the increased expense. The higher cost facilitates a deal that might otherwise not proceed.

Fees and the cost of flexibility

Beyond the interest rate, developers must account for various establishment and ongoing costs. These can include valuation fees, quantity surveyor costs and lender establishment fees. Non-bank lenders may also charge fees for reviewing the feasibility or for rapid approval processes. While these fees add to the upfront cost of the project, they are part of the price for accessing capital outside traditional channels.

Developers should also consider the cost of holding a retention amount. While this money is ultimately released, it temporarily reduces the funds available for the build. The total cost of the facility must be weighed against the project's profitability. The cheapest rate is not always the best outcome if it comes with restrictive covenants that slow down the build or prevent the project from starting at all.

  1. Calculate Total Development Cost (TDC). Sum up all costs associated with the project, including land acquisition, construction costs, permits and professional fees.
  2. Determine Gross Realisable Value (GRV). Estimate the total revenue expected from the sale of the completed project or its end value.
  3. Assess Presale Status. Review the current level of presales or pre-leases, as this significantly impacts the loan-to-value ratio and pricing.
  4. Analyse Exit Strategy. Confirm how the loan will be repaid, whether through a bulk sale, refinancing or individual settlements.
  5. Compare Loan Terms. Evaluate offers from different lenders, considering the interest rate, loan-to-cost ratio and fees.

Common questions

How is interest calculated on a construction loan? Interest is typically charged on the drawn balance only, not the total loan limit. It is often capitalised, meaning it is added to the loan balance and paid at the end of the project.

Why are non-bank construction loans more expensive? Non-bank lenders take on higher risk by offering greater leverage and flexibility on presales. The higher pricing reflects this increased risk tolerance and the bespoke nature of the funding.

What metrics do lenders use to size a loan? Lenders primarily use the Loan to Total Development Cost (LTC) and the Loan to Gross Realisable Value (GRV) to size a facility and assess feasibility.
